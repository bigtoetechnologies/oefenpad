Every image inside a game is a brush drawing in the logo's hand: cobalt ink on bare paper, one idea, no words.

This is the shared style guide for clue images, card faces, scene art, reels, trophies and card backs. It does not cover the painted blocks in the interface (designguide.html), the logo itself, or the 157 photographic word images in assets/wordimages/ — those are placeholders, not the target style.

The per-game briefs say what each image must show. This says how it must be drawn.

Brief What it supplies
voorzetselpaden/*.html Every Voorzetselpaden scene, one card per frame, by CEFR band, with its contrasts and what it must not suggest
story-machine-art.html The 72 reel tiles, by reel and word
This file Stroke, colour, composition, prompt and checklist

Voorzetselpaden uses style A

Since 12 November 2026 every Voorzetselpaden scene is drawn in style A: a flat picture-book pictogram, solid cobalt clothing, simple faces, no shading. The owner chose it over the brush style below because it stays consistent across a batch and reads at the phone choice tile, about 93 CSS px wide.

The brush rules in the rest of this file still describe the other games' art. For Voorzetselpaden they are replaced by the four things in this table.

What Where
The fixed prompt Docs/voorzetselpaden/base-prompt.txt, pasted unchanged
The three references assets/verb-propositions/referenceimages/style-a/; not the files in its origin/ folder
The scenes The cards on each band page, read when the prompt is built: tools/grok/grok.py prompt <band> "<chunk>"
The pipeline Unchanged: Delivered raster art below

One batch is one chunk, in one fresh Grok chat. Attach the three references and paste the prompt that grok.py prompt prints: the base prompt, then the chunk's cards as they are on the page now, then a delivery line naming the zip and every file. Without that line, Grok returns the images inline and they cannot be fetched in bulk. tools/grok/README.html has the whole run.

Review each image against the three references, at full size and at phone tile size, and check the framing: the whole figure, head to feet, at the same scale as the rest of the set. A scene fix goes on its card, not into the base prompt; mark a hand-written scene with the door de eigenaar badge so batch edits leave it alone.

The idea in one line

The logo is the reference. A game image should look like it was drawn by the same hand that painted the O and the P: a few confident, tapered cobalt strokes, visible bristle splits, loose ends, and a lot of paper.

Claim Consequence
The stroke is the line Not a flat vector silhouette, not an icon with a uniform outline
Paper is part of the drawing Transparent background; empty space is composition, not a gap to fill
One idea per image If the silhouette needs a caption, redraw it

Scene art is drawn in the same hand at sketch weight: a bold brush-pen drawing with a wide nib, visible dry-brush streaks and directional hatching — not a thin pen-and-ink illustration and not a pictogram. See Style anchors.

Non-negotiables

These are inherited from decisions.html and designguide.html.

Rule Why
No text, letters or numbers in the image, ever One art set serves every first language; the words are drawn in HTML
Cobalt only, no orange Orange still means "do this now"; the logo's orange dab stays in the logo
No culture-specific or Dutch souvenir imagery The audience is international
No meaning carried by colour alone The drawing must carry the meaning
Readable at 64 px That is a phone-sized clue or reel tile; line weight has to carry it
One idea per image A learner has one glance to connect the picture to the chunk
Transparent, vector where possible It has to sit on paper in both themes
Scene art is 2:3 portrait 512 × 763 shipped; one ratio and one camera keep contrast pairs comparable

The logo's orange accent is brand identity, not an art convention. Do not copy it into a scene. If an image needs an accent, use a heavier cobalt stroke.

Style anchors

Retired for Voorzetselpaden, which uses the style A references above. These three remain the reference for brush-style art in the other games.

Three delivered scenes define the target. Measure a new image against them; if it would not pass unnoticed beside these three, it is not finished.

Anchor What it anchors
wachten-op-vriend.png People: seated, in profile, the fold of a coat and a chair
wachten-op-trein.png Place: rails in perspective, a figure seen from behind
wachten-op-eten.png Hands and objects on a tabletop, in perspective

All three live in assets/verb-propositions/referenceimages/, are 784 × 1168 RGB PNGs with the white paper baked in, and use an ink core of about #0A41A6–#1657C9, hue 218–220°. Measured at 784 px wide:

Measure Target How it was measured
Ink coverage 14–22% plain, to 27% busy share of pixels with min(r,g,b) < 200
Typical stroke 4–8 px 60–65% of the ink survives a 3 px erosion, 30–40% a 5 px erosion
Boldest contour 9–14 px the chair frame, a rail, the edge of a table
Hatching 2–4 px dense directional strokes on folds and under objects
Subject extent scene 90–100% of the width; person 35–55% the person is half the scene, not the whole frame
Filled mass hair, plus one or two small accents clothing, furniture and background stay white inside the contour

Where the coverage figure comes from. The fifty drawn scenes in assets/verb-propositions/incoming/ (one folder per band) measure 13–27% by the definition above, median 18. The three anchors measure 14.0, 15.4 and 18.5. An earlier version of this table said 13–17% with a 20% failure threshold; seventeen of the fifty broke it, and so did one anchor, so the threshold was describing nothing. Coverage scales with how much is in the scene: one person on a bench sits near the bottom of the range, two people with furniture near the top. Judge the drawing against the anchors, not the number — a generator given a ceiling will draw thin to stay under it.

The stroke is a wide-nib brush or chisel marker, not a pen. Each line is a broad band with pressure variation, dry-brush streaks and split bristles; the ink is dark and solid at the core, not pale or washed out. The contour is gone over a second time on one or two edges. Hatching is drawn in quick, dense clusters that read as tone at arm's length, with paper still visible between the strokes.

The fifty shipped scenes went the other way: solid black clothing, big eyes and motion lines. They are too heavy. But a drawing that comes back as a thin, even pencil line has also missed the anchors — the reference line is bold, rough and inky. That is why this is a redraw rather than a tweak.

The brush

At 784 px wide — the delivered source size — these weights hold:

Role At 784 At 512 shipped What it does
Primary contour 9–14 6–9 The shape that must survive at 64 px
Secondary 4–8 3–5 Internal structure, limbs, object parts
Detail and hatching 2–4 1.5–3 Folds, hair, shadow, tabletop lines

The primary contour becomes roughly 1 px at 64 px. Anything thinner than 2 px at 784 is not load-bearing; if the meaning depends on it, draw it at primary weight instead. A contour drawn at 1 px at 784 reads as pencil, not brush.

One edge, drawn twice

The old rule was one stroke per edge. The anchors do not obey it: they go over an important edge a second time, a few pixels off, or leave a pale guide line running beside the final one. That second pass is what makes the result read as a drawing rather than a traced icon.

  • The second pass goes on one or two edges of a shape, never as a symmetrical outline around both sides.
  • It may stop short or overshoot by 3–6 px at 784.
  • It is thinner than the edge it doubles, usually secondary weight.

Taper

Every stroke starts thin, widens around the middle or the point of pressure, and ends thin. The logo's O is a single looping stroke that overshoots its own start; the P has a long descending stem that narrows as it falls.

A uniform <line> or a stroke-width that never changes is a pen line, not a brush. Draw the stroke as a closed, filled shape:

<!-- a tapered stroke, not a line -->
<path d="M40 80 C90 70 140 74 180 64 C140 88 90 92 40 80 Z"/>

Draw with a pressure-sensitive brush in a vector editor and expand the stroke to an outline if that is easier. The delivered SVG must contain the shape, not a dependency on the tool.

Roughness

Roughness is selective. The stroke has a hand, not a noise texture laid over the whole image.

  • Cut two or three narrow bristle splits into one edge of the widest strokes. Run them with the stroke; do not scatter dots.
  • Let the edge wobble by 1–3 units. Do not make every edge equally ragged.
  • Let joins be loose: one stroke can overshoot another by 2–4 units, or stop 1–3 units short at a corner. Closed, mechanical corners read as vector art.
  • Do not close every shape. The logo's frame leaves the corners imperfect.

What not to draw

Form is line, then hatching, then mass — in that order of preference. The anchors are contour drawings: interiors stay white and shadow is built with hatching, not filled in.

  • No solid masses beyond hair and one or two small accents. A figure whose whole coat is painted black belongs to the old fifty, not to this set.
  • No flat vector silhouettes with a uniform outline around them.
  • No thin, even pencil line. The contour is a broad, rough brush stroke with pressure variation, doubled on one or two important edges. If the drawing looks delicate, it is wrong.
  • No symmetrical outline tracing both sides of every shape. A second pass on one edge is a sketch; a second line all the way round is an icon.
  • Hatching is allowed and wanted, in one form only: quick, directional strokes clustered on the shadow side of a fold or under an object. The strokes follow the form, vary in length, and may touch or run together, but the paper must still show inside the cluster and they must not become a solid fill or a cross-hatch. They must read as a tone at arm's length, not as decoration.
  • No gradients, drop shadows, inner shadows, blur, grain or paper-texture overlay. The hatching is drawn, not filtered.
  • No photographic detail. A face is not the subject, but it is not a smiley either; see People and animals.
  • No decoration. Background is allowed only as one place cue — the door, the platform, the window that tells the learner where they are. Never wallpaper, scenery or a full room.

People and animals

The audience is adult, and the drawing is a clue, not a cartoon.

  • Adult proportions. A standing figure is about seven heads tall, a seated one about four. The anchors are drawn from life, not from an icon set.
  • Face in profile or three-quarter: a brow, one eye under it, a nose line, a mouth, an ear. That is the whole face. No second eye bulging at the viewer, no open mouth, no sweat drops, no motion lines around the head.
  • Hair is the one place that carries mass: a filled, brushy shape with a few strokes through it, as in all three anchors.
  • Hands are drawn, not mittened. A thumb and the line of the fingers is enough, as on the table in wachten-op-eten.png.
  • Clothing is contour plus hatching folds, never a black fill. Where the old fifty painted a coat solid, draw the outline, hatch the shadow side and let the paper show.
  • No skin colour. There is one ink.
  • Do not caricature. The person is a learner in an everyday situation, not a type.

Composition

Scene art is 512 × 763, 2:3 portrait. Everything else starts at 512 × 512, viewBox="0 0 512 512".

Rule Detail
Canvas 2:3 portrait for scene art; square for tiles, reels and card faces
Safe area 32 units at 784, 20 at 512; the drawing may cross it, nothing required may fall outside it
Subject extent The scene spans 90–100% of the width; the person is 35–55%; it may touch the left or right edge, with paper kept at top and bottom
Camera One camera for the set: eye level or slightly above. The anchors put the subject in the left half, facing right; reverse it only when the chunk reverses the direction
Place cue One object in the other third completes the sentence — a door, a platform, a window, a table edge
Element count One subject and one place cue. A third object only when the chunk needs it
Focus The thing the sentence is about is the clearest mark, not necessarily the largest
Background Paper, both in light and dark mode
Frame None. The logo's border is the mark, not an image convention
Contrast pairs Design the pair together: same camera, same scale, same composition; change only the meaningful element

At 64 px the lines no longer resolve into a silhouette, so judge the reduced image by whether the idea is still nameable — who is doing what, where. If it is not, the composition is wrong, not the detail. A uniform heavy outline added to save the small size puts the image back in the old style; the answer is a clearer silhouette, not a thicker border.

For a contrast pair, look at the two images side by side at 64 px. Neither may suggest the other's sentence. Designing one and then deriving the other produces near-duplicates with the wrong emphasis.

Colour and background
Light Cobalt #2B50C8
Dark Cobalt #5C7DEB
Measured ink core in the anchors #1657C9, hue 218–219°
Background Transparent. No white plate, no paper-coloured rectangle
Depth Stroke weight, overlap and hatching, not a second blue or a grey

The logo uses one cobalt. Do not add --kobalt-diep or --kobalt-licht to an illustration to create depth; that is a page-fill behaviour, not an art one.

A generated PNG will not land on the token exactly — the anchors sit at a slightly deeper #1657C9. That is fine, because the shipped file is a mask on a cobalt block and never carries its own colour; do not correct the hex in the raster.

Game art has to survive the theme swap without a second file:

  • Preferred: inline SVG with fill="currentColor", and CSS sets color: var(--kobalt). The token swaps with the theme.
  • If the image must be an <img>, use a CSS mask on a cobalt block rather than baking #2B50C8 onto a white background.
  • Never ship a white rectangle behind the drawing. On dark paper it becomes a bright card, and the brush disappears.

Do not use the interface paint filter

data-verf and the #op-verf / #op-verf-klein filters belong to rectangular UI fills. Their baseFrequency is tuned to the 300 × 150 and 100 × 30 viewBoxes, and verf() is applied by JavaScript. Art must render with JS off and at any size.

A static brush filter is allowed if it is tuned to the 512 viewBox and checked at both 512 px and 64 px. The #lijn filter in design-options/i-penseellijn.html is the starting point; retune baseFrequency for 512 rather than copying the 1440-wide values. Geometry with real bristle gaps is more reliable than a filter and is preferred.

Option I was rejected as a home-page composition, not as an illustration style. It is the closest existing visual reference for the brush as a line, and the frame in option H shows the same loose-corner behaviour. Do not re-propose either as a page layout; use them to judge a drawing.

Files and naming

What Where
A game's own art assets/<game>/art/<id>.svg
Story Machine card backs assets/storymachine/art/<id>.jpg (existing)
Story Machine tiles emoji placeholders in server/Oefenpad.Resources/Games/Data/reels.json until drawn
Preposition scenes assets/voorzetselpaden/<scene-id>.png (shipped raster; see Delivered raster art)
Shared art assets/shared/art/<id>.svg

The games themselves live in client/src/games/; art never sits beside the code any more, because only assets/ is served. A picture's id is what the server sends in a round view (for example /assets/wordimages/animals/dog.jpg).

  • One file per idea. Ids are stable, lowercase and hyphenated; they match the data key or scene id.
  • Do not duplicate a shared image into each game. Put it in assets/shared/art/.
  • Raster is a placeholder, not a deliverable. The shipped exceptions are the BigToe mascot card back in assets/storymachine/art/, which is waiting to be replaced, and the thirty preposition scenes described below. New art still starts as an SVG.

The filename is not the alt text. For a picture-clue exercise the image is the question, so do not put the answer in alt; the exercise's prompt carries the accessible name. Whether picture-clue games can be made fully screen-reader playable is a separate accessibility question, not something the artwork can solve.

Delivered raster art

The fifty Voorzetselpaden scenes are the one shipped exception to "SVG, 512 × 512". Batch 1 arrived as 784 × 1168 PNGs; batches 2 and 3 as 1152 × 1712 files; batch 4 mostly at 784 × 1168 with one at 1152 × 1712. All arrived with the white paper baked in and drawn at 2:3 portrait. The originals stay untouched in assets/verb-propositions/archived/, one folder per batch. They are kept for provenance only; the style A references in referenceimages/style-a/ are the only images attached to a Voorzetselpaden generation.

They are not used as they arrived. A baked white background becomes a bright card on dark paper and the brush disappears, so each file is processed into an alpha mask and shipped at assets/voorzetselpaden/*.png, 512 × 763:

# paper -> 0, ink -> 255; the floor keeps near-white noise out of the mask
d = 255 - min(r, g, b)
a = 0 if d <= 10 else round((d - 10) * 255 / 245)

The ink is not recoloured. The mask is drawn on a cobalt block in CSS, so the theme swap happens without a second file and --kobalt stays the only ink:

.beeld{
  background:var(--kobalt);
  -webkit-mask-image:var(--beeld); mask-image:var(--beeld);
  -webkit-mask-size:contain; mask-size:contain;
  -webkit-mask-position:center; mask-position:center;
  aspect-ratio:512/763;
}

Set --beeld inline per element with url("art/<scene-id>.png"). No JavaScript, no filter, and it renders on paper in both themes.

The portrait ratio is no longer an exception: 2:3 is the default for scene art, because all the images have one camera and one scale, which is what makes the contrast pairs comparable. Cropping to squares would cut each scene differently. Scene art starts at 784 × 1168 and ships at 512 × 763. Square 512 × 512 remains the default for tiles, reels, trophies and card faces.

A picture-clue tile must not carry the answer in its accessible name. The choice buttons get aria-label="Beeld 1" and the drawing is aria-hidden; only the Ontdek card, where the image is the content, uses the scene's alt.

Generation prompt

The Voorzetselpaden prompt is Docs/voorzetselpaden/base-prompt.txt; how to use it is under Voorzetselpaden uses style A above. The old brush-pen locked header and its batch protocol are retired. They are in git history (before 12 November 2026) for provenance, and must not be pasted: they ask for hatching the references never had, which is where the drift came from.

Rule Why
One batch per chunk, one fresh chat per batch A chunk's variants are drawn together, so they match
The same three references attached every time The style holds only while the target is in the context
The base prompt unchanged Every change to it needs a fresh test batch before it is trusted
Reject and redo, do not patch A patched image never sits right beside the others

The output is a sketch, not a deliverable. Pipeline it as described in Delivered raster art: alpha mask, 512 × 763, tinted by CSS. Do not ship the raster and do not correct its blue.

Do / Don't

Do Don't
Draw the contour, then hatch the shadow Fill a coat, a chair or a wall black
Draw bold, rough, pressure-varying strokes Draw a thin, even pencil line
Use one weight for the edge and a lighter one for detail Give every line the same thickness
Re-draw one or two edges a few pixels off Outline both sides of every shape
Keep interiors white Shade with a gradient or a filter
Let hatching build a tone Cross-hatch or fill the shadow solid
Let the scene fill the frame and touch an edge Shrink the subject into a card-sized pictogram
Give the frame one place cue Fill the background with scenery
Draw the contrast family in one batch Design one, then tweak a copy
Draw the face in profile, neutral Draw a cartoon face with an open mouth
Use one cobalt Add a second blue, grey or orange for depth

Checklist

  • [ ] A Dutch speaker can name the target sentence from the image alone, without the English gloss.
  • [ ] The idea is still nameable at 64 px.
  • [ ] It passes unnoticed beside the three anchors at full size.
  • [ ] Ink covers 14–22% for a plain scene, up to 27% for a busy one; most lines are 4–8 px at 784, bold contours 9–14 px.
  • [ ] The line is a bold brush band with dry-brush streaks, not a thin even pencil line.
  • [ ] Interiors are white; only hair and one or two small accents are filled.
  • [ ] Shading is directional hatch clusters that read as tone, with paper visible inside them.
  • [ ] Faces are in profile with a brow, one eye, nose, mouth and ear, and a neutral expression.
  • [ ] The scene spans 90–100% of the width; the person is 35–55%; paper is kept at top and bottom.
  • [ ] There is exactly one place cue, and it carries the chunk's location.
  • [ ] The contrast pair is clearly different side by side, and neither image suggests the partner.
  • [ ] No text, letters, numbers, logos or signage with readable marks.
  • [ ] Cobalt only; no orange.
  • [ ] 2:3 portrait, 784 × 1168 delivered, 512 × 763 shipped as an alpha mask.
  • [ ] Flat white background, no frame and no shadow around the image.
  • [ ] Renders in light mode, dark mode and with JavaScript off.

Why the old word images do not set the style

The 157 images in assets/wordimages/ are photorealistic white-background product shots. They were inherited with the vocabulary games and are useful as placeholders, but they are the opposite of the logo's hand: full colour, full frame, no line, no paper. Do not add more in that style.

Replacing them is a separate, larger job. Until then, new art follows this guide, and a mixed set is a known limitation rather than a reason to dilute the style.
