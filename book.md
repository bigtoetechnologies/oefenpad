# Chapter 1 — You already understand Dutch (well, a little at least!)

Before you learn a single new word, here's a gift: your ear already recognises a huge slice of Dutch. The spelling can look strange — *uit*, *huis*, *moeder* — but say them out loud (the Dutch 'ui' sounds like 'ow' in English 'cow' and Dutch 'oe' like 'oo' in 'moon') and English peeks through: *out*, *house*, *mother*.

Dutch and English are cousins. They split apart roughly 1,500 years ago, and the most-used words in both languages still carry the family resemblance. There are hundreds, if not thousands, of words that are similar enough in both langauages to be instantly recognisatble. This chapter gives you the first 101 most common of those sound-alike pairs.


## You can already read Dutch

Read this out loud, slowly:

> *Mijn moeder en vader drinken water. Mijn broer is in het huis. De kat slaapt op het bed. De zon is warm, de wind is koud. Ik kan zwemmen, zingen en dansen.*

Not sure how to say it? Here's a pronunciation guide for that sentence — the same words spelled the way an English speaker would sound them out:

> *Mine mooder en farder drinken varter. Mine broor is in het house. De kat slahpt op het bed. De zon is varm, de vint is kowt. Ik kan zvemmen, zingen en dansen.*

Did you understand it? Almost certainly yes — and you haven't been taught a single word yet. That paragraph is twenty-five Dutch words long, and your English-language ear just decoded nearly all of them on autopilot.

This chapter is about turning that accident into a skill. We're going to walk through six everyday rooms of vocabulary, climbing gently from words that are already English to words that need a tiny sound-shift to click into place. The full 101 sound-alike list lives in **Appendix A** at the back of the book; the predictable sound-shifts between English and Dutch — the *th → d*, *ght → cht*, *ou → ui* patterns and their friends — are explained in **Appendix B — Pronunciation and Sound-Shifts**. Dip into either whenever you want more depth.

## What just happened

You didn't memorise a hundred words. You walked through six everyday rooms of vocabulary and noticed, again and again, that most "new" Dutch words are maybe just English words wearing a small predictable disguise. Actually I like to think that Dutch came first and it's the English words wearing the disguise! That's the whole trick of Dutch for an English speaker: a handful of tiny sound-shifts unlock hundreds of words you'd otherwise call foreign.

You'll have spotted some of those shifts already — *th* flattening to *d* (*moeder*, *vader*, *broer*), *ght* hardening to *cht* (*nacht*, *dochter*), *s* turning into *z* (*zon*, *zomer*, *zingen*), *f* into *v* (*vader*, *vallen*, *vuur*). **Appendix B — Pronunciation and Sound-Shifts** collects them all in one place, with extra examples, so you can use it as a cheat-sheet whenever a Dutch word looks stranger than it is.

The full 101 sound-alikes are waiting in **Appendix A** if you want to see the whole catalogue at once.

## Beware the false friends
However, while there are so many words that are similar enough to be useful, there are also words that sounds similar, or even identical, but have very different meanings. Even some of those in the table above can sound like other words with different meanings. For example, arm is the same in both English and Dutch, but, the duth word 'aarm' sounds very similar but actually means 'poor'. This is where the infamous Dutch long-vowels can be important to recognise.

Here are the traps hiding inside the 101 you just read. The Dutch word means one thing; the English word it sounds (or looks) like means something else entirely. Don't let your ear fool you.

| Dutch word | Really means (Dutch)              | Sounds like (English) | But that means…           |
|------------|-----------------------------------|-----------------------|---------------------------|
| kind       | child                             | kind                  | nice; a type or sort      |
| brood      | bread                             | brood                 | offspring; to worry       |
| peer       | pear                              | peer                  | an equal; to look closely |
| hart       | heart                             | hart                  | a male deer               |
| oor        | ear                               | oar                   | a rowing paddle           |
| vrij       | free                              | fry                   | to cook in hot oil        |
| stoppen    | to stop — *and* to put/insert *and* to darn socks | stop  | only "to stop"            |
| branden    | to burn                           | brand                 | a logo or trademark       |
| arm        | arm — *and* poor                  | arm                   | only the body part        |

The lesson: sound-alikes get you 90% of the way, but always check the sentence around the word. Dutch is generous with its loans to English — it just keeps a few jokers in the deck. We will look more closely at false friends in chapter 4. 

## One word, several jobs

**Note:** The meanings you already recognise are correct — Dutch just stretches some words further than English does. This isn't a Dutch peculiarity; English does it too (*light* = not heavy / not dark / a flame). The list below is less a warning than a heads-up: when a familiar word doesn't fit the sentence, assume it has a second job you haven't met yet.

| Dutch word | The meaning you already know | Also means…                                       |
|------------|------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| stoppen    | to stop                      | to put / insert ("*stop het in je tas*"); to darn socks |
| maken      | to make                      | to fix, to repair ("*de fiets maken*")            |
| vinden     | to find                      | to think, to have an opinion ("*ik vind dat…*")   |
| leren      | to learn                     | to teach; and as a noun, *leather*                |
| horen      | to hear                      | to belong ("*dat hoort bij jou*")                 |
| zien       | to see                       | to look; "*ik zie het wel*" = "we'll see"         |
| koken      | to cook                      | specifically *to boil*                            |
| bakken     | to bake                      | also to fry (eggs, bacon, anything in a pan)      |
| hard       | hard                         | also *loud*, and *fast*                           |
| warm       | warm                         | also *hot* (Dutch barely distinguishes the two)   |
| lang       | long                         | also *tall* (for people)                          |
| noot       | nut                          | also a *musical note*                             |
| branden    | to burn                      | the noun *brand* in Dutch means *fire*            |
| trap / trappen | stairs (noun)            | to kick; to pedal a bike ("*trappen op de fiets*") |

The pattern to remember: when a familiar Dutch word doesn't fit the sentence, don't assume you misheard — assume the word is doing a second job you haven't met yet.

## Dutch says what it sees

Here's another gift hiding in plain sight. Where English reaches for a Greek root or a French loanword, Dutch usually just glues two everyday words together and calls it done. A kettle? That's a *waterkoker* — a "water cooker." A vacuum cleaner? *Stofzuiger* — a "dust sucker." A hospital? *Ziekenhuis* — a "sick house." A glove? *Handschoen* — a "hand shoe." This isn't as strange as it sounds, we do the same in English, such as "hair brush", "hand soap", but the Dutch do it a lot more often and a lot more literally, looking at you "water cooker"!

Once you notice the pattern, hundreds of Dutch nouns stop being vocabulary to memorise and start being little riddles you can solve on the spot. Here are 101 of them, grouped by where you'll meet them.

These are the words that will trip you up in real conversations, signs, and menus. Here are the 101 most common, ordered roughly by how often the Dutch word appears in everyday Dutch.

# Appendix B — Pronunciation and Sound-Shifts

Dutch and English split apart roughly 1,500 years ago, and most of the words that still look "strange" in Dutch aren't strange at all — they've just been put through one or two predictable sound-shifts. Learn the shifts, and the spelling stops being a wall. This appendix collects the shifts in one place so you can flick back whenever a word looks harder than it really is.

## The three big lenses

These are the three highest-leverage patterns. Between them they explain a huge slice of the vocabulary in Chapter 1.

**Lens 1 — English *th* becomes Dutch *d* or *t*.**
English kept the breathy *th*; Dutch flattened it centuries ago.

> that → *dat*  ·  think → *denken*  ·  thank → *dank*  ·  thousand → *duizend*  ·  brother → *broer*  ·  mother → *moeder*  ·  father → *vader*  ·  tooth → *tand*

**Lens 2 — English *gh* / *ght* becomes Dutch *ch*.**
The English *gh* is silent or hidden; the Dutch *ch* is the throaty sound that's still doing the original job — made at the back of the mouth, a bit like clearing your throat politely.

> night → *nacht*  ·  daughter → *dochter*  ·  light → *licht*  ·  right → *recht*  ·  eight → *acht*  ·  laugh → *lachen*

**Lens 3 — English *ou* (as in *house*) becomes Dutch *ui*.**
The Dutch *ui* is a sound English doesn't have — somewhere between "ow" and "oy" — but it does the same job as English *ou*.

> out → *uit*  ·  house → *huis*  ·  mouse → *muis*  ·  loud → *luid*  ·  south → *zuid*

## Smaller shifts worth knowing

These won't unlock quite as many words as the big three, but you'll see them constantly.

- **English *s* ↔ Dutch *z*** at the start of a word: *sun → zon*, *summer → zomer*, *sing → zingen*, *sister → zuster*, *sea → zee*, *salt → zout*, *sweet → zoet*.
- **English *f* ↔ Dutch *v*** at the start of a word: *father → vader*, *fall → vallen*, *fire → vuur*, *friend → vriend*, *five → vijf*, *fish → vis*.
- **English *k* stays hard in Dutch** where English softened it to *ch*, *c* or *ck*: *cook → koken*, *book → boek*, *make → maken*, *cat → kat*, *drink → drinken*.
- **English *y-* ↔ Dutch *j-*** at the start of a word (spelled differently, sounds the same): *year → jaar*, *young → jong*, *yes → ja*.
- **English *-y* ↔ Dutch *-ig* or *-ing*** at the end: *honey → honing*, *busy → bezig*.
- **Dropped *-e***: English often keeps a silent final *e*; Dutch drops it. *Name → naam*, *ride → rijden*, *ice → ijs*.

## Dutch consonants that trip English speakers

- **w** is pronounced like an English *v*: *water* sounds like *varter*.
- **v** is softer than English *v*, halfway between *v* and *f*: *vader* sounds somewhere between *farder* and *varder*.
- **g** and **ch** are both that throaty back-of-the-mouth sound. *Goed* doesn't start like English *good* — it starts with a light scrape.
- **j** is pronounced like English *y*: *ja* = *yah*.
- **r** is usually rolled or tapped, not the smooth English *r*.
- **sch** at the start of a word is *s* + the throaty *ch*, said quickly: *school* sounds like *sk* followed by a scrape.
- A final **-d** is pronounced *t*: *goed* ends with a *t* sound, not a *d*.
- A final **-n** after *-e* is often swallowed in casual speech: *drinken* sounds more like *drinke*.

## Putting it together

Combine the lenses and the vowel cheat-sheet, and most Dutch words you meet in this book can be decoded on sight. When a word looks strange, ask: is there a *th* hiding as *d*? A *gh* hiding as *ch*? An *ou* hiding as *ui*? Once you have the habit, Dutch spelling stops being an obstacle and starts being a clue.
